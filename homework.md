## Homework Guidelines

Each week has one homework, tied to that week's topic (some weeks, it may be related to your
final project instead). Each class day also has a short set of reading questions on that day's
assigned readings, due before class — see the [Weekly Cadence](resources.html#weekly-cadence)
for the schedule. Homework and readings are distributed via Google Drive; both are handed in on
Canvas.

Homework is one open-ended question — not a "learn to code this" exercise. The point is to
practice reasoning about an approach and cheaply testing it, not to produce polished,
production-quality code.

Work through it in three steps:

1. **Think it through yourself first.** Before looking anything up, reason about how *you*
   would address the question. Write this down — it's the part that's actually being assessed.
2. **See how others have addressed it.** Look at the relevant prior work or literature. How
   does it compare to your own idea? What did you miss, or get right?
3. **Code up a toy example to test it.** Use Claude, GPT, or your tool of choice to quickly
   implement a small-scale version of your approach — a couple hours of work, not a full
   system. See what happens.

A few things to keep in mind:

- **Using an LLM to write the code is expected and encouraged** — coding assistance is fine for
  anything in this course. The reasoning in steps 1 and 2 is the part you should do yourself.
- **The best outcome is often a surprising failure, not a clean success.** If your first
  approach doesn't work the way you expected, that's usually more interesting than if it does —
  explain what went wrong and why. Homework is graded on the quality of your reasoning and
  diagnosis, not on whether your first idea worked.
- **Keep the toy example small.** A few hours of work on a scaled-down version of the problem
  is enough to get a real answer; you don't need a large dataset or a long training run.

## What to Submit

A **one-page write-up**, plus your code:

- **Your initial approach** (step 1): a paragraph or two on how you'd address the question,
  written before you looked anything up.
- **What you found in prior work** (step 2): a paragraph on how it compares to your own idea —
  what you got right, what you missed.
- **What you tried and what happened** (step 3): a short description of your toy experiment,
  1-2 supporting plots/numbers if relevant, and — most importantly — your diagnosis of *why* it
  worked or didn't.
- **Code**: a link to a notebook or script. It should run, but it doesn't need to be clean —
  we're reading it to check what you actually tried, not reviewing it as production code.

One page for the write-up itself (code/plots don't count against that limit). If your diagnosis
in step 3 is genuinely the interesting part, spend most of the page there rather than padding
out steps 1-2.
